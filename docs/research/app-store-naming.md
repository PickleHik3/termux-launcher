# App naming and store visibility (Google Play, Apple App Store, F-Droid)

**Question (verbatim):** "app naming practices for google play store (such online stores) visibility"

**Dated summary answer (2026-10-22).**

- Hard rules (primary sources): the Google Play app title is at most 30 characters, with no emoji, no repeated special characters, no ALL CAPS except a brand, no ranking or award claims, and no promotional words ("Free", "No Ads"). The short description is at most 80 characters, the full description 4,000. Apple's name and subtitle are 30 characters each, keywords 100 characters. F-Droid reads a 50-character title, 80-character summary and 4,000-character description from the repo.
- Google says the title should be unique, focused and avoid common terms, and that search ranks apps on "ratings, reviews, downloads, and other factors" with unpublished weights. It gives no weight for the title. Two Google help pages disagree on whether extra keywords help: one says they "will not impact ranking", another says to use SEO practices in the description.
- Apple states that text relevance (title, subtitle, keywords, category) plus user behaviour (downloads, ratings, reviews) drive search. It is the only store that documents the title as a ranking input.
- Peer-reviewed evidence on names is thin and mostly about Apple's store. The one study of title length with a causal-style design (Apple, 2015 data, 1,849 apps in the tables) found longer titles, counted in words, were associated with a higher chance of being in the top-300k chart on one date, with no significant further gain beyond about 8 to 11 words (roughly 50 to 70 characters). A 50,000-app Google Play crawl found name length essentially uncorrelated with downloads (Spearman -0.007). A 2025 UK Play Store study (n=1,964) found description features, not the title, were the top predictors of ranking for the title's own keyword. No study found compares brand-only with descriptive names.
- For this project: keep the name short (well under 30 characters), put the function words in the title or short description once, never stuff, and treat the Termux brand overlap as an impersonation and trademark risk to check against Play's Impersonation policy before any Play submission.

## 1. What the stores require (primary sources)

Pages fetched 2026-10-22. The Google pages show no revision date beyond "(c) 2026 Google"; treat the quoted text as current on that date.

**Google Play**
- Title: "Your app title must be 30 characters or less." "Make sure your title is unique, accessible, and avoid common terms." "Words like 'Free' and 'No Ads' promote deals and don't belong in app titles or developer names." "Don't use CAPS unless your brand is capitalized." ([Best practices for your store listing](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13393723))
- Metadata policy: no emojis, emoticons or repeated special characters in the title, icon or developer name; no text indicating store performance or ranking ("App of the year", "#1"); "Avoid using repetitive or unrelated keywords or references"; "Don't use special characters or all caps to manipulate search ranking"; metadata that is "misleading, improperly formatted, non-descriptive, irrelevant, excessive, or inappropriate" is not allowed. ([Metadata policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9898842))
- Limits: app name 30, short description 80, full description 4,000 characters; "Repetitive or irrelevant use of keywords in the app name, description, or promotional description" can lead to suspension. ([Create and set up your app](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9859152))
- Short description: "A short description should convey your app or game's message in 80 characters or less", summarise "your app's biggest benefits", don't repeat it in the full description. Full description: "Users may only read the first few sentences." Guidance is "everyday language, not a list of keywords." ([Best practices](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13393723))
- Discovery: "Apps are ranked based on a combination of ratings, reviews, downloads, and other factors"; weights are proprietary. "Long titles may be shortened on some devices." "Use SEO best practices, but avoid keyword spamming and impersonation." Put the most important description text in the top half of the page. Avoid subtle misspellings of general terms. ([Get discovered on Google Play search](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/4448378))
- Conflict inside Google's pages: the short-description page says unnecessary keywords added to improve search "will not impact ranking and provides a bad user experience" ([Add preview assets](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9866151)), while the discovery page tells developers to apply SEO practices in the description. Read together: description text is indexed, keyword spam is penalised, no marginal weight is published.
- Impersonation: titles and icons "so similar to those of existing products or services that users may be misled" are prohibited, as are apps that "falsely claim to be the official app of an established entity". The fetched text has no explicit exception for unofficial or fork apps, and the page was truncated. ([Impersonation](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9888374)) The Intellectual Property policy is framed around likelihood of confusion (search-result summary only; not opened in full).

**Apple App Store**
- Guideline 2.3.7: unique name, accurate keywords, no "trademarked terms, popular app names, pricing information, or other irrelevant phrases just to game the system"; "App names must be limited to 30 characters"; subtitles "should not ... reference other apps, or make unverifiable product claims." ([Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); page date not shown; the last 14k characters were not read.)
- Name and subtitle are each at most 30 characters ([App information reference](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information)). Keywords: 100 characters, comma-separated, avoid plurals of included words and category names; promotional text "doesn't affect your app's search ranking" ([Product page](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/product-page/)).
- Ranking: "text relevance (matches for your app's title, subtitle, keywords, and primary category), as well as user behavior (downloads, ratings and reviews, and more)" ([Search](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/search/)). Apple advises "a simple, memorable name that is easy to spell and hints at what your app does", and putting the value explanation in the subtitle.

**F-Droid** ([Descriptions, graphics and screenshots](https://f-droid.org/docs/All_About_Descriptions_Graphics_and_Screenshots/), [Inclusion policy](https://f-droid.org/docs/Inclusion_Policy/))
- Fastlane-style `title.txt` (max 50), `short_description.txt` (max 80, mandatory), `full_description.txt` (max 4,000, mandatory); the title is not mandatory. A fork "must receive corresponding name, icon and string changes" and a new application ID. The pages state no ranking rules.
- Obtainium and GitHub releases have no store search; naming there matters only for web search and the repo's own metadata. No primary document and no study found on that.

## 2. Does the title length or keyword content affect visibility? (literature)

- **Chiba et al. 2026 [full text, PDF]**: Apple App Store, random sample of apps observed in 2015, before the title limit (the abstract says 1,998 apps; Tables 2 and 6 report 1,849 and the estimates use 1,848, a discrepancy the paper does not explain). Outcome: whether the app was in the top-300,000 of the Global Rank chart on 15 December 2015, a visibility proxy, not downloads. Treatment is title length in words (about 6 characters per word, so 5 words is roughly 30 characters). Method: multivalued treatment effects (propensity-score weighting plus regression adjustment, the "EIF" doubly robust estimator) and a cheap-talk game model. Analysis 1 (Tables 2 to 5): 1,510 apps had titles of 5 words or fewer (raw chart probability 0.19), 197 had 6 to 8 words (0.32), 142 had more than 8 words (0.46). Adjusted probabilities (Table 4, EIF): 0.201, 0.270, 0.428. Effects against the shortest group (Table 5, EIF): +0.069 (CI 0.003 to 0.134) and +0.227 (CI 0.141 to 0.312); more than 8 words versus 6 to 8 words: +0.158, CI excludes zero. Analysis 2 (Tables 6 to 8): splitting the long group into 9 to 11 words (88 apps) and more than 11 words (54 apps) gave adjusted probabilities 0.377 and 0.470; the more-than-11 versus 9-to-11 difference of 0.093 had a CI overlapping zero, and so did 9-to-11 versus 6-to-8 (+0.105, CI -0.012 to 0.222). So the pre-limit data support gains past 5 words but cannot say whether they continued past about 11 words (the top group is only 54 apps). The authors conclude Apple's 30-character limit may hurt consumers because informative keyword titles helped users. Controls: update history, age, size, developer scale, price, in-app purchase, languages, ratings, category (Table 2). Stated limits: matching cannot rule out endogeneity; one platform, one year; title syntax and images not studied. Not controlled (my observation): the brand strength of the words in the title, which could confound long titles of popular apps. Second pass: this replaces my first-pass reading ("over 30 / 50 characters"); the groups are in words.
- **Strzelecki 2020 [full text, PDF]**: 49,990 Google Play apps and 6,040 App Store apps crawled in May 2019. Average name length 23 characters on both; Spearman correlation of name length with downloads -0.007 on Google Play (Table 5); no significant correlation with reviews or rating on the App Store (Table 4). Play downloads track review count (rho 0.600). The "developer-dependent" factor list (name, subtitle, descriptions, etc.) is asserted from store documentation and prior work, not tested; the paper is inconsistent about Play's name limit (30 in the text, 50 in Table 1). Names and descriptions were "not fully utilized".
- **Abrar and Herzog 2025 [full text, PDF]**: UK Google Play, 1,964 non-gaming apps (final set), supervised classification of an app's search rank (top 10 for its own extracted title keyword: High, Medium, Low). Best model SVM, 75% accuracy. Strongest features for the High class were description sentiment and keyword density in the description; the title keyword and its presence in the short description were among the "ASO-controlled" features; installs, ratings and reviews also mattered. Limits: imbalanced rank classes, UK only, correlational, rank measured only for the app's own title keyword, so it does not show whether a different title word would have ranked better.
- **Karagkiozidou et al. 2019 [full text, PDF]**: PRISMA review (Science Direct, Springer, Google Scholar, February and March 2018, papers from 2010 to 2018): 614 citations, 472 unique, 75 full-text screened, nine included (Fig. 1), plus web whitepapers. None of the nine included studies tests title wording. In its Table 1 the "Application Name & Title" criterion (C7) is supported by one source only, a Moz marketing blog post (Sefferman 2016); the nine articles concern installs, ratings, reviews, upgrades, simulation and fraud. C7 claims (section 4.7): the title is "one of the most important factors", keywords in it should match many queries, and "Usually, up to 255 characters can be used"; only the first 23 characters on the App Store and 30 on Google Play are visible. The 255 limit is out of date (section 1). The ranking traits it establishes are downloads, ratings and reviews (4.1 to 4.3). Following its references reached no earlier title study: Tian et al. 2015 (IEEE ICSM, 10.1109/icsm.2015.7332476, abstract only) studies 28 factors of rating, not visibility, and finds app size, promotional images and target SDK most influential; the others are simulation, fraud and review-system papers (two Lim and Bentley items model ranking algorithms). Not opened: Sefferman 2016 and Ganguly 2013 (blogs).
- **Bilal et al. 2025 [abstract only]**: Google Play Education apps; machine-learning themes extracted from titles and descriptions; "Math and Homework Support" and similar functional themes correlate with success (downloads and ratings over time plus sentiment). Suggests functional words in titles and descriptions help in that category. Full text not accessible (preview only).
- **Barlow et al. 2019 [full text, PDF]**: 139,582 Google Play apps from 83,115 first-time developers (first app published March 2015 to July 2016), 589,681 app-month observations, outcome installs; generalised estimating equations; description similarity measured by cosine similarity to the top-100 apps of each of 41 categories (exemplar) and to a category prototype. Finding: installs are highest for descriptions highly similar to an exemplar and dissimilar to the category prototype; the authors say such developers are nine times more likely to reach 100,000 or more installs (a level under 4% of apps reach). It measures description text only and never examines names or titles. The authors state that Google Play's search draws on the developer-provided text description (introduction; the footnote behind the claim was not checked) and that they cannot observe the search algorithm or control for top-app and "similar to" listings. Limits: English descriptions only, one platform, and category contrast cannot be fully excluded as an alternative explanation. Relevance here: the tested lever is distinctive-but-on-topic description wording, not title wording.
- **Hu et al. 2020 [full text, PDF; abstract and introduction read, rest skimmed]**: app squatting measurement; 10,553 apps with names or package names confusingly similar to top-500 Google Play apps, 51% flagged malicious by VirusTotal, present in 33 markets including Google Play. Shows why the stores police similar names, and why a derived name carries takedown risk.

**Where evidence agrees:** informative, keyword-bearing but non-spammy titles helped on Apple (Chiba; Apple's own text-relevance statement); downloads, ratings and reviews dominate rank on both stores (Strzelecki; Abrar; Google and Apple pages); keyword stuffing is policed on both.
**Where it conflicts or is weak:** the claim that the title is a major ranking factor on Play rests on practitioner sources (Karagkiozidou's only support is a blog) and on Apple's own statement for Apple; title length helped on Apple in 2015 (Chiba), yet name length shows no relation to Play downloads in 2019 (Strzelecki, a plain correlation with no controls); Google's own pages are inconsistent on keyword value; there is no controlled or experimental evidence on Google Play title wording. All studies are observational.

## 3. Brand versus descriptive naming, subtitle, short description

- Apple: the name should "hint at what your app does" and be distinctive; use the subtitle for value; avoid generic terms ([Product page](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/product-page/)). Google: unique and focused, avoid common terms ([Best practices](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13393723)). Both ask for a distinctive name plus a descriptive supporting line, which is a brand-plus-function pattern, not a documented ranking formula.
- Barlow's tested lever is description distinctiveness; no tested study isolates brand-only versus descriptor names.
- Chiba describes the pre-2016 practice of "brand name plus most important keywords" and its payoff on Apple; Strzelecki describes the same pattern as commonly observed (description, not tested).
- Google Play has no subtitle; the 80-character short description plays that role and is text Google says is indexed, with no published weight.
- No peer-reviewed study found comparing brand-only, descriptive-only and brand-plus-descriptor names on visibility.

## 4. Implications for Termux Launcher (reasoned from the sources, not tested)

- Title: "Termux Launcher" is 15 characters, leaving room within 30 for a descriptor, but nothing found shows added words move Play rank; the stores' own advice is a short, distinctive name.
- Short description (80 characters) and the first two sentences of the full description are what Google says users read and that are indexed; write them in everyday language, once.
- Brand overlap: Google's Impersonation policy bars titles and icons that could mislead users about a connection to another app or developer. A name containing "Termux" is that shape of risk. Read the full policy and FAQ, and check the Termux project's trademark position, before any Play submission (not verified here).
- F-Droid: a fork must carry its own name, icon and application ID.
